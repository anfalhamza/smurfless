            <div class="category">CLINICAL UPDATE</div>
            <h1>SMuRFless STEMI: Emerging Evidence and Clinical Implications</h1>
            <p class="article-meta">Written by <strong>Dr Anfal Hamza</strong></p>
            <p class="article-meta">Topic: SMuRFless Myocardial Infarction / STEMI · Updated September 2026</p>
        </div>

        <div class="article-content">

            <h2>Introduction</h2>
            <p>
                Acute myocardial infarction is traditionally associated with a well-established group of cardiovascular risk factors, commonly referred to as standard modifiable cardiovascular risk factors (SMuRFs). These include hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and cigarette smoking.
            </p>
            <p>
                However, a clinically important proportion of patients presenting with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) have none of these conventional risk factors. This group is commonly described as having SMuRFless STEMI.
            </p>
            <p>
                The recognition of SMuRFless STEMI challenges the assumption that the absence of traditional risk factors equates to a low risk of coronary events. In fact, several studies have reported substantial short-term morbidity and, in some cohorts, higher mortality among patients presenting with STEMI without conventional risk factors.
            </p>

            <h2>How common is SMuRFless STEMI?</h2>
            <p>
                The reported prevalence varies considerably depending on the population, study design, and definition used.
            </p>
            <p>
                In the Australian GRACE and CONCORDANCE registries, approximately 19% of patients with first-presentation STEMI and no previous cardiovascular disease had no SMuRFs. Interestingly, the proportion increased from approximately 14% to 23% over the study period.
            </p>
            <p>
                A large US National Inpatient Sample analysis identified more than 115,000 SMuRFless STEMI admissions, representing approximately 26.6% of the STEMI population studied.
            </p>
            <p>
                These differences highlight an important issue: the exact prevalence of SMuRFless STEMI depends heavily on how patients and risk factors are defined.
            </p>

            <h2>Do SMuRFless patients have better outcomes?</h2>
            <p>
                Surprisingly, the answer may be no.
            </p>
            <p>
                Several observational studies have reported worse early outcomes in SMuRFless STEMI despite the absence of conventional risk factors.
            </p>
            <p>
                A systematic review and meta-analysis involving 521,150 patients with first STEMI found that SMuRFless patients had higher in-hospital mortality compared with patients with at least one SMuRF. The pooled odds ratio for in-hospital mortality was 1.43, while cardiogenic shock was also more frequent in the SMuRFless group.
            </p>
            <p>
                Similarly, a US National Inpatient Sample analysis reported higher odds of in-hospital mortality among SMuRFless STEMI patients, with an adjusted odds ratio of approximately 1.67. Acute renal failure was also more frequent.
            </p>
            <p>
                However, the evidence is not completely uniform.
            </p>
            <p>
                A pooled individual-patient analysis of cardiac magnetic resonance studies found no significant differences in infarct characteristics or clinical outcomes between SMuRFless and SMuRF-positive STEMI patients.
            </p>
            <p>
                Another contemporary PCI cohort found no significant difference in 30-day outcomes and reported lower long-term mortality among SMuRFless patients.
            </p>
            <p>
                The important message is therefore not that SMuRFless STEMI invariably has a worse prognosis, but that the absence of traditional risk factors does not guarantee a favorable prognosis.
            </p>

            <h2>What might cause SMuRFless STEMI?</h2>
            <p>
                The mechanisms underlying SMuRFless myocardial infarction remain incompletely understood.
            </p>
            <p>
                Traditional cardiovascular risk factors do not account for all pathways involved in atherosclerosis and acute coronary thrombosis. Proposed contributors include:
            </p>
            <ul>
                <li>Genetic susceptibility</li>
                <li>Inflammation and immune-mediated mechanisms</li>
                <li>Thrombogenic factors</li>
                <li>Endothelial dysfunction</li>
                <li>Environmental exposures</li>
                <li>Psychosocial and mental-health-related factors</li>
                <li>Epigenetic mechanisms</li>
                <li>Previously unrecognized metabolic abnormalities</li>
            </ul>
            <p>
                A recent state-of-the-art review emphasizes that the pathogenesis of SMuRFless acute myocardial infarction remains poorly defined and may involve multiple non-traditional biological and environmental pathways.
            </p>
            <p>
                Importantly, SMuRFless does not mean risk-free. A patient may have clinically relevant cardiovascular risk that is not captured by the four conventional SMuRF categories.
            </p>

            <h2>Coronary anatomy and presentation</h2>
            <p>
                SMuRFless patients can present with severe coronary disease despite apparently favorable baseline risk profiles.
            </p>
            <p>
                In a study of young STEMI patients aged ≤60 years, 17.4% were classified as SMuRFless. These patients had somewhat shorter and less complex coronary lesions than patients with SMuRFs, but they experienced higher rates of in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events and procedural complications.
            </p>
            <p>
                Other studies have reported differences in pre-PCI coronary flow and clinical presentation, including a higher frequency of cardiogenic shock in some SMuRFless populations.
            </p>
            <p>
                These findings suggest that coronary anatomy alone may not explain the clinical behavior of SMuRFless STEMI.
            </p>

            <h2>A potential treatment gap</h2>
            <p>
                One of the most clinically important observations is that SMuRFless patients may receive less evidence-based secondary prevention.
            </p>
            <p>
                The systematic review and meta-analysis found that SMuRFless patients were less likely to receive statins and ACE inhibitor/ARB therapy at discharge compared with patients who had conventional risk factors.
            </p>
            <p>
                This raises an important clinical concern.
            </p>
            <p>
                A patient who has suffered an acute myocardial infarction remains a patient with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, regardless of whether hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, or smoking was present before the event.
            </p>
            <p>
                Therefore, the absence of SMuRFs should not be interpreted as the absence of secondary-prevention needs.
            </p>

            <h2>What should clinicians take away?</h2>
            <ol>
                <li><strong>Do not equate absence of risk factors with absence of cardiovascular disease</strong><br>
                    A patient without hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, or smoking history can still develop STEMI.</li>
                <li><strong>Treat STEMI according to established acute coronary syndrome principles</strong><br>
                    SMuRFless status should not delay reperfusion or other evidence-based acute management.</li>
                <li><strong>Assess the patient beyond the traditional SMuRF framework</strong><br>
                    Family history, body composition, metabolic health, inflammatory conditions, lifestyle, psychosocial factors, and other relevant clinical characteristics may provide additional information.</li>
                <li><strong>Do not overlook secondary prevention</strong><br>
                    After MI, cardiovascular risk remains substantial even when conventional risk factors were absent before presentation.</li>
                <li><strong>Recognize that the evidence is evolving</strong><br>
                    Different studies have produced different estimates of prevalence and prognosis. Large prospective studies are still needed to clarify mechanisms, risk stratification, and optimal long-term management.</li>
            </ol>

            <h2>The Future of SMuRFless MI Research</h2>
            <p>
                SMuRFless STEMI represents an important gap in our understanding of coronary disease.
            </p>
            <p>
                Future research needs to move beyond simply asking whether traditional risk factors are present or absent. Greater emphasis should be placed on biological phenotyping, genetics, inflammation, coronary plaque characteristics, thrombosis, metabolic health, and individualized risk prediction.
            </p>
            <p>
                The ultimate goal is not simply to identify patients as "SMuRFless," but to understand why myocardial infarction occurred and how future cardiovascular events can be prevented.
            </p>

            <div class="bottom-line">
                <h3>Bottom Line</h3>
                <p>
                    SMuRFless STEMI is not rare, and the absence of traditional modifiable cardiovascular risk factors does not eliminate the possibility of severe myocardial infarction or adverse outcomes.
                </p>
                <p>
                    Current evidence suggests that some SMuRFless populations experience higher early mortality, cardiogenic shock, or procedural complications, although other studies have reported similar or even better longer-term outcomes. This heterogeneity emphasizes the need for better definitions, prospective research, and individualized risk assessment.
                </p>
                <p>
                    <strong>For clinicians, the key message is simple:</strong><br>
                    No traditional risk factors does not mean no cardiovascular risk.
                </p>
                <p>
                    SMuRFless STEMI should therefore be recognized as an important clinical and research phenotype requiring continued investigation.
                </p>
            </div>
